# GOQ Museum: developer intentions

Every game exhibited in the GOQ Museum, with its developer's own words about what they intended. Generated from `museum/museum-pack.json` on 2026-10-20.

**How to read this file**

- One section per game, oldest episode first (by air date).
- **Metadata** is a short list of fields. Empty fields are left out.
- **Summary** is a one-paragraph summary of the developer's intention (the piece's `intention` field), not the developer's own words.
- **Developer answers** are the developer's own words, verbatim (only the doubled quote marks from the original form are cleaned up), answering the same three questions:

  1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?
  2. Why did you choose this experience?
  3. Were there specific moments, mechanics, or ideas you built around that emotional goal?

- A missing answer is marked *(no answer)*.

## 1. Cave Escape

- **Developer:** Popitch
- **Aired:** 2025-12-26
- **Category:** The Shape in the Dark
- **Also fits:** The Long Road to Mastery
- **Described by visitors as:** "the one with the spooky scary skeletons"
- **Episode:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VMHBlHE2EQ
- **Game:** https://popitch.itch.io/cave-escape
- **Piece ID:** `piece-um9vhkk`

### Summary

Martin made Cave Escape as a focused study in level design and mechanical progression. He was working through the specific craft of teaching players without tutorials, ramping difficulty without frustration, and fusing precision platforming with light combat. The game builds toward moments of unease in the cave environment, and the final escape sequence is designed to test everything the player learned while racing against rising lava. But the vision seems driven by what Martin wanted to practice making rather than by a feeling he wanted to express.

### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

The game emphasizes exploration and gradual progression, with challenges that steadily increase in difficulty. Mechanics are taught organically through gameplay, without explicit tutorials. The narrative is minimal, designed to create unease in a dark cave environment, heightened by the absence of music. In the final sequence, the player’s skills are fully tested as they race to escape the cave while rising lava adds pressure and urgency. The experience is meant to end with a strong sense of accomplishment and relief.

**Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

I was inspired by platformers such as Celeste, HK and Messenger, and wanted to create a complete game on my own. The project was focused primarily on level design and coding practice rather than story or visuals. I really enjoyed working on level design and consistently tried to see the game from the player’s POV, gradually increasing the difficulty of each challenge without making it overwhelming or frustrating.

**Q3. Were there specific moments, mechanics, or ideas you built around that emotional goal?**

I may have already touched on this in an earlier answer, but to be more specific, my goal was to create fluid movement with tight controls so the difficulty could ramp up and be managable. Unlockable abilities and sword upgrades strengthen the sense of progression, while subtle background color changes visually reinforce it. Although the game is linear, I hope I maintained a feeling of exploration throughout.

## 3. Survivor TD

- **Developer:** Cartable Studio
- **Aired:** 2026-01-09
- **Category:** Mad Scientist
- **Described by visitors as:** "the one where you lure enemies through towers instead of defending"
- **Episode:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4zjXBWOGxCk
- **Game:** https://store.steampowered.com/app/3811540/Survivor_TD__Favor_of_the_Gods/
- **Piece ID:** `piece-8e4ay05`

### Summary

Cartable Studio blended survivor games with tower defense to create something new and prove their commitment to game development. They wanted players to feel smart and creative by discovering efficient builds and hidden mechanics like wall knockback damage, starting weak but growing powerful over time. The emotional arc was meant to build toward excitement and frustration that makes players want another run.

### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

I want the player to try and think about the best possible maze he could create. He has three different " traps" and four towers and it would be cool if he would think about how to use them the most efficiently.

There's also a "hidden" way of doing damage, if he pushes ennemies on walls, they will receive damage so the more he chooses power ups that buffs his knockback strength the more it does damage. I would like the player to feel the excitement of discovering a new build path by experimenting mechanics that aren't explicitly said.

I also want the player to feel pretty useless at the start and becoming better during runs and through meta progression. ( basic roguelite i guess)

An intended design is also that the player shall start by running around evading ennemies, but the more his maze is efficient the more he can just stay still, so the games kinda starts as a Survivor and finishes as a Tower Defense. Though, since I shortened the demo to not give too much game time, it's only possible to some extent.

Finally, this is a demo, so if there's a little frustration because the player can't play his run anymore after the boss fight, then it's good. It would mean he wants to see more.

**Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

Why making a game ? 

I started the project two years ago.

I was formerly a student in GameDesign, but as I went through the years, I wanted to see other fields before commiting to a full GD career. Now, I'm older and feel confident that I want to do Game dev.

So, I'm making this game because I try to find jobs in the Video game Industry and in my experience, there's no better way than a solid project to show.

Why this kind of game ?

Because, I really enjoyed Survivor genre and it seemed not so hard to devellop ( boy I was wrong ), though I wanted to offer a different kind of Survivor genre and then I came up with this idea of mixing both TD and Survivor genres. 

Which was... a pretty hard idea to design because as a player when playing Survivor games you like to drawn all the ennemies together and explode all of them in one blow or deleting the waves with many powers, here it's not possible because of the Tower Defense aspect where ennemies has to follow some paths.

It took me 1 year and a half to finally find it fun to play.

**Q3. Were there specific moments, mechanics, or ideas you built around that emotional goal?**

The feelings are not quite well finished, I need to work more on the balancing to have a feeling of progression throughout the same run. Also, the never ending polish to make the game juicy enough to be cool to play.

## 8. Hope Timbre

### Summary

Peter built Hope Timbre from watching his own son's experience with music therapy and sound-based treatment. He wanted players to feel a 'wow effect' with each puzzle level and—crucially—to feel empathy for two siblings trying to heal their world. The core mechanic of gathering items from dreams to use in reality embodies his belief that even in imagination, there is hope and strength to change things. ### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

I wanted the player to feel the wow effect when playing each level - each is different in terms of puzzles, decorations and music. We use adaptive music in a way that each time a part of puzzle is solved, music changes a little and at the end of each stage the music is evolved to the maximum. As the game is about collecting instruments and songs in dreams and using them to heal a broken world of two little siblings, I wanted players to feel empathy for the little ones and the urge to help them. **Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

My son is ill with several diagnosis, he needs physiotherapy among other things. Music was always a very entertaining and soothing thing for him. I restricted this to specific points only. The mechanic to gather items in dreams and use them in the world where children live -  I wanted the player to realize that even in dreams and music, you can find hope and strength to change things.

## 9. Diecast

- **Developer:** sofamire
- **Aired:** 2026-03-05
- **Category:** Mad Scientist
- **Described by visitors as:** "the one where you build dice like a deck builder"
- **Episode:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6hdykkgWaGw
- **Game:** https://store.steampowered.com/app/3861370/Diecast_Demo/
- **Piece ID:** `piece-ct78svp`

### Summary

Sofamire made Diecast as a version of Yahtzee designed to be fun to play repeatedly. The core idea is that players should feel dopamine hits from big numbers and from discovering overpowered builds. Unlike a traditional deck-builder where probability converges as you modify your deck, Diecast resets probability with each roll, asking players to build strategy around dice that have fixed but specific behaviors (odd-only, Fibonacci sequences, copied values) and score categories they can buy and upgrade.

### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

I wanted the player to feel engaged and excited about building different strategies as they go through a run, having a build they’re going for, but also the excitement of changing a build on the fly. I wanted the player to feel there’s a possibility that their build isn’t good enough and may not get them through to the end.

**Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

Diecast is a game I made for myself first, because I wanted to make a version of Yahtzee that was fun to play and a surprise every time. It supposed to be an engaging solitaire-style experience with added depth. It’s like a mechanical puzzle, finding which dice, items, and score work together, and then deeper than that finding ways to game the system (make extra money, sell off scores that won’t be used for early cash flow, etc., waiting to end a level to get a few more hands in (leveling them up further in the process).

**Q3. Were there specific moments, mechanics, or ideas you built around that emotional goal?**

Dopamine hits from big numbers, but also from finding an overpowered build.

## 10. Warlord's Penance

- **Developer:** Zizaco
- **Aired:** 2026-03-20
- **Category:** The Long Road to Mastery
- **Described by visitors as:** "the one where blocking still damages you slightly"
- **Episode:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L5GAeWL7HB0
- **Game:** https://zizaco.itch.io/wl-penance
- **Piece ID:** `piece-uw8z1nn`

### Summary

The developers built Warlords Penance around combat that feels accessible but requires players to think strategically rather than reactively. They wanted to create a approachable dungeon crawler that avoids the complexity, grinding, and wiki-dependency of classic games in the genre. Each mechanic—the cooldown system, partial damage from regular blocks, adaptive difficulty—was designed to create pressure and engagement while remaining fair.

### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

I want the player to feel constantly pressured, while feeling engaged with the continuous progress (unlocked doors, getting a new ally, getting close to the exit). I want the player to feel a slight relief after every enemy encounter they overcome. We're trying to make it so the game is not unfair, but far from being an "easy" walk in the park.

**Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

I wanted to try a simple yet engaging combat system.
Plus, I love the idea of classic dungeon crawlers, but I think they're too difficult to approach/enjoy (stats, complexity, grindy, need wiki, etc). Also I love old school dark fantasy setting (which once again is reserved to few games that are not easily approachable).
We wanted to make a game that is simple to make, simple to play, and could be expanded all while still being approachable. :)

**Q3. Were there specific moments, mechanics, or ideas you built around that emotional goal?**

- Atmosphere: Visuals. I would love feedback on the visuals. We wanted to go with something that is easy to produce (we're a 2-guys team, we have to make it feasible), all while being good enough to drive players looking for a more serious tone.
- Atmosphere: Sound Design. Music that adapts when in combat (or when with low-health). Sound design that provide good feedback and sense of danger (while not being "horror-like").
- The combat system that is easy, but that keep you at your toes.
- Adaptative difficulty (like Resident Evil), the game is engaging for beginners, but also for experienced players.

- What's next? For the extended commercial release we're planning to add more content (dungeons levels), equipment, such as better swords, shields, bows, and a simple skilltree. These wouldn't be super complex, just to provide a more streamlined sense of progress. Let me know your thoughts

## 14. Ominoflux

- **Developer:** acvd
- **Aired:** 2026-04-30
- **Category:** The Long Road to Mastery
- **Also fits:** Mad Scientist
- **Described by visitors as:** "the one where you have to prioritize gems over clearing lines"
- **Episode:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UfPQxJmhC_A
- **Game:** https://store.steampowered.com/app/2917340/Ominoflux/
- **Piece ID:** `piece-p0gsjux`

### Summary

The developer built Ominoflux around achieving a flow state through mastery of layered, interconnected systems. They wanted players to experience a game that feels hectic and difficult at first, then becomes genuinely fun once understood—where clearing lines is mere survival and gem collection and stat optimization are the true path to victory. The flux mechanic, spawning blocks, gem timing, and combat are designed to work together cohesively, but the game was developed in isolation. The developer prioritized creating something genre-pushing and unique as opposed to another Tetris clone.

### Developer answers

**Q1. What were you hoping the player would feel while playing your game?**

Similar to Tetris, I am hoping the player could enter a certain "flow state". 

I want the player to feel that the game is hectic, difficult and nuanced at first, but once mastered becomes really fun - even easy!

I want the player to feel pride in mastering the layered mechanics, and be able to show off skills and perform clutch plays.

**Q2. Why did you choose this experience?**

For the game, I set out a number of non-negotiable things:

1 - To avoid any genre that is trending (vampire survivor like, slot like, etc.)
2 - To avoid any genre that is over saturated (platformer, horror, etc.)
3 - To avoid anything that required heavy graphics (visual novels, RPGs, etc.)

I grew up playing puzzle games, these are games wracking your brain until you get that Eureka moment is the main attraction, but puzzles do appear in other genres as well, like horror, and I'll be honest, I don't like the way they're used, even in AAA games like the Resident Evil series, or more AA stuff like Poppy Playtime, it feels borderline disrespectful.

It's usually some variation of: "Get chased by a monster" then "do some arbitrary puzzle that barely connects to anything else" then repeat Ad nauseam.

I do understand why this is the case though, horror and puzzles are opposites by design.

Horror relies on keeping the player in the dark, both thematically and literally, but also mechanically, as well as keeping the player powerless, you're usually in a dimly lit, unfamiliar place, not knowing if you're alone or not,  or whether you have enough bullets (assuming you even get a gun) to fight whatever is lurking in the dark, after all, a horror game immediately looses it's scare factor once you know how everything works, or you have enough control over the situation.

Puzzles on the other hand, are the exact opposite, by design, they are required to communicate all the information the player could possibly need, in order for them to be solvable.

"Here's where you are, here's the exit, here's how everything works and how to manipulate it, good luck."

What if the monster was both a threat to be overcome, but also a puzzle to be solved?"

Well, the general idea is to take something that can be a source of fear in a horror game, then turn that into a resources you have to manage in order to solve puzzles.

I've always been a fan of the "Weeping Angel" mechanic in horror games, those monsters that only move if you're not looking at them (usually a statue or mannequin of some kind, something that isn't supposed to be able to move), games like SCP: Containment Breach made it popular with the SCP-173 monster, and now AAA games like Resident Evil: Shadows Of Rose and even Minecraft with it's new monster "The Creaking" have started implementing this type of behavior.

I always thought this kind of enemy had a lot of potential, as it created interesting situations for the player to navigate, and the fact the player had limited control over it made it even more interesting, I thought it was the perfect way to test this little design hypothesis, by making a puzzle game, where this horror monster, is the main mechanic.
