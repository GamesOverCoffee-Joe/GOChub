# Polish the museum: plan

Joe, 10/21. Nothing in the museum is broken. The player can polish it, entirely by choice, and while they work, visitors come to them for recommendations. "Polish the museum," not "fix the museum."

## The idea in one paragraph

Volunteer chores become gentle, tactile care: vacuuming the carpet, polishing case glass, mopping, freshening the trash cans, watering the plants. None of them has a correct state or a fail state. What you do stays visible (stripes in the carpet, gleaming cases) and slowly wears down as people use the museum, so there's always something to do on the next visit. While you're working, visitors walk up and ask for something to see. That's where recommending happens: they come to you.

## What we're keeping in mind

- **Mission:** a deeper connection with GOQ. Polishing is texture; recommending is the center. Every chore should give a visitor a reason to come over, or draw people to a piece.
- **Expression, not tasks:** stripe patterns, what you choose to make gleam, where you spend your time. No checklists, no "you missed a spot."
- **Recognition, not pay:** people notice your work ("someone did the carpet in diagonals today"), not a coin pop.
- **Calm:** no day dial in the default game. The day's rhythm (tours, rushes, closing) still happens, just without a countdown.

## Phases

### Phase 0: the Polish Lab (test room) — started
A test room you reach with a button code, so mechanics can be tried one at a time on the game page without touching the museum.
- **Code:** Down, Down, Down, Up, Up, Up, Start (keyboard: the same arrows and Enter). The same code takes you back to where you were; so does the lab's door.
- **In the room:** a big carpet, two visitors wandering, and the tools as they're built.

### Phase 1: vacuuming — prototype built (in the lab)
- Two vacuums, each on its own stand, yours once you pick them up (like Zelda items): the **wide vacuum** brushes all nine tiles around you (yours too), the **hand vacuum** just the tile you're on (for corners and around benches).
- **Equipment** (Start menu, once you own one): a row of icons; pick which you hold. They work in the lab for now.
- **Hold B** to vacuum: the tiles under the vacuum remember the direction you went, showing light and dark stripes like a mowed lawn. **Double-tap B** to leave it on; tap B to turn it off.
- **Finishing the floor:** when every carpet tile you can reach is freshly done, soft twinkles across it (the same ones as a polished case), and the people there notice (a heart, a line). No counter.
- **Closing a room for cleaning:** a velvet rope stand by the entrance. A on it: a dip to black, everyone's out. A again: they come back. In the museum this becomes one stand per wing entrance (tours out too, the reader stays); these can't be recommended.
- The lab has three cases, two benches and carpet samples (each wing's carpet) to try it on.
- No correct pattern. Straight lines, diagonals, checkerboards, initials.
- Visitors walking across the carpet scuff your stripes, but only a minute after you vacuumed there, and slowly. Vacuum again any time.
- **To decide after trying it:** how strong the stripes look; how fast scuffs wear them; whether the vacuum slows you down; a hum or sparkle as you go.

### Phase 2: polish — prototype built (in the lab)
- The **polishing rag** (Equipment): hold B at a case and the rag rubs the glass. No fingerprints, just polish.
- Over time a case's glass dulls: its piece looks a little less saturated. Polished, it's brighter, and a fully polished case glints now and then.
- The glint used to be what a fully read piece did; reading no longer sparkles, polishing does (curator Staff: Glint on polished cases).
- The **watering can** is in Equipment too (B at a plant).
- **To decide after trying it:** how fast cases dull (four minutes now, for testing); paintings too; replacing today's "wipe the fingerprints" fix in the museum.

### Phase 3: mopping and trash cans — prototype built (in the lab)
- The **mop** (Equipment): hold B as you walk the wood floor. It leaves a wet sheen that dries in about six seconds; visitors step around it while it's wet.
- The lab's **trash can** fills slowly as people walk past (two minutes or so). A swaps in a fresh bag; the can wobbles as the lid settles. It never overflows into a problem.
- Also this round: tools are held like a coffee cup; A no longer waters plants (the watering can does, with B, everywhere; it hangs in the staff room).
- **To decide after trying it:** does the mop need a "wet floor" sign; how fast cans fill in the museum (from visitors' cups); whether the bag swap needs a sound.

### Phase 4: plants as care — prototype built (in the lab)
- Four stages: tired, normal, full, flowering. Water a plant once a day (the watering can, B) and it grows a stage the next day.
- After flowering it goes back down through full to normal, then grows again: a cycle, not a finish line.
- A day without water and it slips a stage; it never gets worse than a little tired, never dies.
- In the lab a day is one minute, so you can watch it happen. Watered today: the soil looks damp. Growing: a soft glint.
- **To decide after trying it:** how a day maps in the museum (a real day, or the game's day); whether visitors notice a flowering plant (Phase 6); replacing today's dying-plant fix.

### Phase 5: visitors come to you
- While you're working (holding a tool, or just finished), a nearby visitor sometimes walks up: "Excuse me, do you work here? I want something cozy." Their ask can be vague, so knowing the collection is the skill.
- You answer with what you're thinking of (Select), as today's recommending.
- Try it in the lab with the two visitors first.

### Phase 6: care draws attention
- What you polish gets noticed: visitors drift to a gleaming case, linger, take photos. Your care quietly steers people toward the pieces you love.
- People mention your work: comments on the carpet pattern, a coworker who likes your stripes, a photo of your checkerboard.

### Phase 7: into the museum
- Move what worked out of the lab: carpets in the hallways and café, the cases, the plants, the bins.
- Retire the old fix-type chores (dust, crooked frames, fingerprints, the jammed can, dying plants) and the day dial in the default mode.
- Wear comes from the day itself: tours and rushes scuff and smudge more.
- Saving: what you polished stays for the day.
